Every notion of "closeness" you have met so far — convergence, limits, continuity — was defined in terms of a **distance function**. That works beautifully in $\mathbb{R}^n$, but many spaces that arise naturally in mathematics and computer science have no sensible notion of distance at all. **Topological spaces** let you keep all of that theory while throwing out the distance function entirely.

## The key insight from metric spaces

In a [metric space](../../../elementry/math/metric_space/) $(X, d)$, a set $U \subseteq X$ is called **open** when every point in $U$ has an [open ball](../analysis/ball/) around it that fits entirely inside $U$:

$$
U \text{ is open} \iff \forall x \in U,\ \exists r > 0 : B(x, r) \subseteq U.
$$

Once you have this collection of open sets, you can re-express every major concept — convergence, continuity, connectedness — using *only* membership in open sets. The metric itself disappears from the statements.

This is the insight: the open sets are doing all the work. The distance function is just one way to generate them.

## The axioms of a topology

Pause and ask: what properties does the collection of open sets in a metric space actually have? You can verify three facts for any metric space:

1. The empty set $\varnothing$ and the whole space $X$ are both open.
2. Any union of open sets (even an infinite one) is open.
3. Any **finite** intersection of open sets is open.

Note that infinite intersections are *not* required to be open: in $\mathbb{R}$, the intersection $\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} \left(-\tfrac{1}{n}, \tfrac{1}{n}\right) = \{0\}$ is a single point, which is closed, not open.

These three facts are now taken as **axioms**. A topology throws away everything except what these three properties need.

**Definition.** A **topology** on a set $X$ is a collection $\tau \subseteq \mathcal{P}(X)$ of subsets of $X$ satisfying:

$$
\text{(T1)} \quad \varnothing \in \tau \text{ and } X \in \tau, \tag{1}
$$

$$
\text{(T2)} \quad \text{if } U_\alpha \in \tau \text{ for all } \alpha \in I, \text{ then } \bigcup_{\alpha \in I} U_\alpha \in \tau, \tag{2}
$$

$$
\text{(T3)} \quad \text{if } U_1, \ldots, U_n \in \tau, \text{ then } U_1 \cap \cdots \cap U_n \in \tau. \tag{3}
$$

The pair $(X, \tau)$ is called a **topological space**. The elements of $\tau$ are called the **open sets** of the topology.

Every metric space is automatically a topological space: take $\tau$ to be the collection of all sets that are open in the metric sense — the three axioms hold, as you verified above.

## Three fundamental examples

### The discrete topology

Let $X$ be any set, and let $\tau \coloneqq \mathcal{P}(X)$ — every subset of $X$ is declared open. This is the **discrete topology**. You can check T1–T3 immediately: unions and finite intersections of subsets are subsets.

The discrete topology is the "finest" possible topology on $X$: it has the most open sets. It corresponds to the discrete metric ($d(x, y) = 1$ for $x \neq y$), where every singleton $\{x\}$ is an open ball.

### The indiscrete topology

At the other extreme, let $\tau \coloneqq \{\varnothing, X\}$ — only the empty set and the whole space are open. This is the **indiscrete topology** (also called the *trivial* topology). T1 is satisfied by definition; T2 and T3 reduce to easy cases with only two sets.

The indiscrete topology is the "coarsest" possible topology: it has the fewest open sets. It is not generated by any metric on more than one point.

### The metric topology

Given a metric space $(X, d)$, the collection

$$
\tau_d \coloneqq \{ U \subseteq X : \forall x \in U,\ \exists r > 0 : B(x, r) \subseteq U \}
$$

is a topology on $X$, called the **metric topology** induced by $d$. This is the bridge between metric spaces and topology spaces: every metric space "is" a topological space via this construction, but not every topological space comes from a metric.

## Open and closed sets

A subset $C \subseteq X$ is **closed** if its complement $X \setminus C$ is open.

Note carefully: "closed" is *not* the opposite of "open" in common parlance. Sets can be:

- Open but not closed: $(0, 1)$ in $\mathbb{R}$ with the standard metric topology.
- Closed but not open: $[0, 1]$ in the same topology.
- Both open and closed (*clopen*): $\varnothing$ and $X$ are always clopen. In a disconnected space, other clopen sets exist too.
- Neither open nor closed: $[0, 1)$ in $\mathbb{R}$.

The axioms T1–T3, translated to closed sets via De Morgan's laws, say:

- $\varnothing$ and $X$ are closed.
- Any **finite** union of closed sets is closed.
- Any intersection of closed sets (even infinite) is closed.

This is a useful dual picture that you will see repeatedly.

## Comparing topologies on the same set

Two different topologies can live on the same set $X$. If $\tau_1 \subseteq \tau_2$ — every open set of $\tau_1$ is also open in $\tau_2$ — then $\tau_2$ is said to be **finer** than $\tau_1$ (and $\tau_1$ is **coarser** than $\tau_2$). Finer means more open sets; more open sets means a more refined notion of "closeness."

The discrete topology is finer than every other topology on $X$. The indiscrete topology is coarser than every other topology on $X$.

In general, two topologies on the same set need not be comparable: neither may be a subset of the other.

## Why abandon the metric?

The payoff is generality. Here are two spaces that have natural topologies but no obvious metric:

- **The Zariski topology** on an algebraic variety, where open sets are complements of the zero-sets of polynomials. This is central to algebraic geometry and, via algebraic $K$-theory, eventually touches type systems and programming language semantics.
- **The Scott topology** on a partially ordered set, where open sets capture "upward-closed sets closed under directed joins." This topology is the mathematical foundation for **denotational semantics** — the very framework used to give a precise meaning to recursive programs.

By working in the generality of topological spaces you can talk about continuity, convergence, and compactness in all of these settings with a single set of definitions.

## Summary

- A **topological space** $(X, \tau)$ is a set $X$ together with a collection $\tau$ of *open sets* satisfying three axioms: $\varnothing$ and $X$ are open (T1); arbitrary unions of open sets are open (T2); finite intersections of open sets are open (T3).
- Every **metric space** gives rise to a topological space by taking all metrically-open sets as $\tau$.
- The **discrete topology** ($\tau = \mathcal{P}(X)$) is the finest; the **indiscrete topology** ($\tau = \{\varnothing, X\}$) is the coarsest.
- A set is **closed** when its complement is open; sets can be open, closed, both (clopen), or neither.
- One topology is **finer** than another when it contains more open sets.
- The abstraction strips distance away, leaving only the open-set structure — which is enough to define continuity, convergence, and connectedness in far more general settings.
